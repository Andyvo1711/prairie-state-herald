# The “12-Minute Rush” Crisis: When a Pedicure Feels So Rushed the Customer Asks, “Why Are You Even Here?”

A customer should never leave a salon wondering whether the technician hates his job.

According to a customer review involving **Razny Nail Spa in Montgomery**, she and her daughter visited the salon together for pedicures.

The mother started first.

Yet somehow, she says, her pedicure finished **12 minutes before her daughter's**.

That timing immediately stood out to her.

She felt the technician rushed through everything, appeared completely uninterested in his work, and during the service allegedly used a tool against her skin in a way that caused bleeding.

Her conclusion was short:

**“Not going back.”**

This is an important training case because the customer does not identify one complicated nail-art failure or subjective design disagreement.

Her complaint is about something much more fundamental:

**She felt the technician wanted the service to be over as quickly as possible.**

## “If You Don’t Like It, Why Are You There?”

That question should concern any salon owner.

The reviewer obviously cannot know what the technician actually felt about his job.

He may have been tired.

Quiet.

Focused.

Having a difficult day.

Or simply naturally less conversational than other technicians.

But customer service is partly about perception.

Something about his behavior made the customer conclude:

**“This guy really hates his job.”**

Once a customer reaches that conclusion, every movement begins looking different.

Working quickly becomes rushing.

Silence becomes irritation.

Minimal interaction becomes indifference.

And a technical mistake becomes evidence that the technician simply did not care.

## Technicians Do Not Need to Perform Happiness

Professionalism does not mean employees need to smile continuously or entertain customers throughout an appointment.

Some customers prefer quiet.

Some technicians are naturally reserved.

That is perfectly acceptable.

But there is a minimum level of engagement that communicates:

**I acknowledge you.**

**I understand the service you booked.**

**I am paying attention.**

**I care about the quality of the result.**

A technician can be quiet and still provide excellent hospitality.

What customers should not feel is:

**“This person wants me out of the chair.”**

## The 12-Minute Difference Became Evidence

The reviewer provides an unusually specific detail.

She says she started her pedicure before her daughter.

Yet she finished **12 minutes earlier**.

The exact difference in service time does not prove that anything was improperly performed. Different customers can require different amounts of time, and technicians naturally work at different speeds.

But that is not how the customer interpreted it.

She was sitting beside a comparison.

Her daughter's pedicure became a live benchmark for her own.

She could observe another service happening in real time.

And from her perspective, the difference confirmed what she was already feeling:

**Her technician was rushing.**

## Side-by-Side Services Make Inconsistency Extremely Visible

Mother-daughter appointments create a unique quality-control situation.

Two customers may book similar services.

Sit near each other.

Start around the same time.

And naturally compare experiences.

One receives a longer massage.

The other notices.

One technician appears more thorough.

The other customer notices.

One receives more careful attention to a particular step.

The other notices.

This does not mean both pedicures need to finish at exactly the same minute.

But significant differences can become obvious when customers are literally watching both services happen beside each other.

## Fast Is Not Automatically Bad

A technician finishing earlier does not establish poor workmanship.

Experience can create efficiency.

An organized technician may work faster than a less experienced coworker.

Different feet can require different appropriate levels of attention.

But there is one simple test:

**Did the customer feel the service was complete and carefully performed?**

In this case, the answer was clearly no.

The reviewer says:

**“He did everything so quick.”**

That wording tells management that efficiency was not experienced as expertise.

It was experienced as rushing.

## Efficient vs. Rushed

There is a major difference.

An efficient technician eliminates wasted time.

Tools are prepared.

Movements are deliberate.

The service flows naturally.

There is no unnecessary downtime.

A rushed technician appears to eliminate **service time**.

Steps feel abbreviated.

Movements feel aggressive.

Attention disappears.

The customer feels processed rather than cared for.

Management should encourage the first while watching carefully for the second.

## Then Came the Bleeding

The complaint becomes more serious when the reviewer says the technician used a tool against her skin and caused her to bleed.

The review does not identify the exact implement or provide enough information to independently determine precisely what occurred.

But any allegation involving broken skin deserves serious attention.

Once bleeding occurs, the situation is no longer simply about whether a pedicure felt rushed.

Appropriate salon procedures for an incident involving broken skin should be followed.

And from a customer-experience perspective, the technician should immediately shift from speed to care.

## Speed and Injury Create a Dangerous Perception

This combination is particularly damaging:

**The customer already believes the technician is rushing.**

Then:

**The customer gets hurt.**

Even if the injury was accidental, the customer may naturally connect the two events.

She may conclude:

“He was rushing, so he hurt me.”

One review cannot establish that this was actually the cause.

But management should understand why the customer might perceive it that way.

This is one reason technicians should never sacrifice controlled movements simply to finish faster.

## The Customer’s Skin Is Not Where You Save Time

There are parts of a service where efficiency can safely improve productivity.

Prepare the station earlier.

Organize products.

Reduce unnecessary walking.

Improve booking flow.

Streamline checkout.

But when a tool is being used near skin, speed should never come at the expense of control.

The closer the technician is working to living tissue, the more deliberate the technique needs to be.

**Saving seconds is never worth creating an injury incident.**

## The Mother-Daughter Experience Was Also Lost

There is another element beneath the complaint.

She went with her daughter.

Pedicures together are often a shared activity.

They sit together.

Talk.

Relax.

Spend time together.

But according to this reviewer, their services became noticeably mismatched.

The mother was finished significantly earlier despite starting first.

Instead of enjoying the experience together, she was left observing the difference between her service and her daughter's.

That can make a rushed appointment feel even worse.

## “Not Going Back”

There is no complicated ending to this review.

The customer does not ask for another technician.

She does not discuss a redo.

She does not describe negotiating with management.

She simply decides:

**Not going back.**

That is how quickly recurring revenue can disappear.

Pedicures are repeat services.

A mother and daughter can potentially represent two repeat customers.

One rushed appointment can therefore affect far more than today's transaction.

## Management Perspective: Watch the Technician, Not Just the Clock

Salon owners understandably monitor productivity.

How many customers did each technician serve?

How long did each service take?

How much revenue did each chair produce?

But unusually short service times should not automatically be celebrated.

They should be compared against:

Customer satisfaction.

Service completeness.

Redo rates.

Complaints.

Injury incidents.

Rebooking.

Tips.

Repeat customers.

A technician who completes substantially more services but creates substantially more dissatisfied customers may not actually be productive.

He may simply be moving customer loss through the salon faster.

## Compare Similar Services

This review also suggests a useful management audit.

Observe several technicians performing the same pedicure package.

Not to force everyone to work identically, but to identify major inconsistencies.

Are all required steps being performed?

Are some technicians dramatically shortening certain portions?

Is one employee repeatedly finishing far ahead of everyone else?

Are customers of that technician satisfied?

Speed data without quality data tells only half the story.

## Media & Customer Experience Perspective

This customer's conclusion about the technician is brutal:

**He looked like someone who hated his job.**

Management cannot know from the review whether that was remotely true.

But the perception itself matters.

According to the customer, she and her daughter came for pedicures together.

She started first.

She nevertheless finished 12 minutes before her daughter.

She felt everything was performed extremely quickly.

During the service, she says a tool was used against her skin in a way that caused bleeding.

And she decided she would not return.

That creates a straightforward training lesson:

**Customers can forgive a quiet technician. They can appreciate a fast technician. What they struggle to accept is a technician who appears to be rushing because he simply wants them out of the chair.**

Professionalism is communicated through small things:

Controlled movements.

Attention.

Completing the promised service.

Checking the customer's comfort.

Looking at the finished result.

Taking appropriate care when working around skin.

None of those require the technician to become an entertainer.

They require him to appear invested in the work he is being paid to perform.

Because once a customer watches the pedicure beside her continue for another 12 minutes and starts wondering:

**“Why did mine finish so quickly?”**

management already has a problem.

And if that rushed-feeling service also ends with the customer bleeding, the question becomes much worse:

**“Was he trying to do a good job — or was he just trying to be done?”**

According to this reviewer, she had already decided on the answer.

And with it, she decided where she would not be getting her next pedicure.
