Religious health care providers have won temporary protection in federal court from parts of Illinois' new medical aid in dying law, which took effect September 12, according to a legal alert from the law firm Hinshaw & Culbertson.

The challenges focus on provisions, tied to the state's Health Care Right of Conscience Act, that the plaintiffs say compel providers who oppose the practice to inform patients about it, refer them, or otherwise facilitate it. They argue this violates free speech and free exercise of religion. Participation in aid in dying is voluntary under the law, and institutions may bar the practice by clinicians acting for them.

In one case, filed August 11 by the Thomas More Society, plaintiffs include four physicians, Springfield Bishop Thomas J. Paprocki and Lutheran Care Center. On August 21, Judge Franklin U. Valderrama entered a stipulated temporary restraining order, meaning the state agreed to it, that barred enforcement against the named plaintiffs and Catholic health care entities in the Diocese of Springfield.

On October 5, the court replaced that order with an amended one. Added plaintiffs include the Illinois Catholic Health Association, the Christian Medical and Dental Associations, the National Association of Catholic Nurses and the Catholic Psychotherapy Association. The order now also covers any provider or entity that belongs to a plaintiff association, though members must give IDPH or the Illinois Department of Financial and Professional Regulation written proof of membership when relying on it. The state agreed not to impose liability for covered conduct from September 12 forward.

A separate suit was filed September 3 by the Becket Fund for Religious Liberty on behalf of Cardinal Blase Cupich, the Carmelite Sisters for the Aged and Infirm, the Chicago Province of the Little Sisters of the Poor and a pharmacist from Morrison. On September 11, the court exempted those named plaintiffs from the law's requirements while the case proceeds. Counsel indicated it is likely to be consolidated with the Springfield case.

Hinshaw cautioned that the orders cover only the specified plaintiffs and members, so institutions that will not participate should adopt written policies.
