Illinois finished fiscal year 2026 collecting roughly $1 billion more in tax revenue than lawmakers anticipated when they wrote the budget last May, according to the final revenue report of the year from the Commission on Government Forecasting and Accountability, as reported by Capitol News Illinois.

The fiscal year ended June 30. The commission's revenue manager, Eric Noggle, wrote that revenues exceeded the budget estimate by $1.003 billion, or 1.8 percent, and noted it was the second straight year of record collections.

## Where the money came from

Noggle attributed the gains mainly to stronger-than-expected personal income tax, estate tax and transfers in. Those gains more than offset weaker performance from the corporate income tax and federal sources.

The estate tax was a standout. Receipts rose $268 million from a year earlier, from $603 million to $871 million. Noggle wrote that the increase reflects strong investment performance among higher-income taxpayers in recent years, though the revenue source is highly volatile. Gov. JB Pritzker had indicated he might support estate tax reform this year, but no change emerged from the legislative session.

## Not a windfall

Capitol News Illinois cautioned that the overperformance is not a surprise pot of cash. Lawmakers had already approved more than $830 million in supplemental fiscal year 2026 spending as revenues climbed late in the session, and an extra receipting day in June accounts for much of the remainder.

The Pritzker administration has a habit of setting conservative initial estimates and then benefiting when collections come in higher, the outlet noted.

## A look at FY 2027

Noggle also flagged a surge of more than $300 million in receipts on the final receipting day of the year. That boost to June, he wrote, may have shifted revenue that would otherwise have arrived in early July, potentially dampening fiscal year 2027 growth.

The new fiscal year's $55.9 billion budget expects less revenue than the final $56.3 billion total for fiscal year 2026. Observers are watching whether the state's conservative estimating continues to leave room, or whether federal tax and policy changes hit state coffers as hard as Democrats have warned.
