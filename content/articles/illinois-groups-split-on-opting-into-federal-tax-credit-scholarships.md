Gov. JB Pritzker has not said whether Illinois will opt into the federal tax-credit scholarship program created by the One Big Beautiful Bill Act, and groups across the state are divided, Chalkbeat Chicago reported on Oct. 13.

The program starts Jan. 1. Individual taxpayers can donate up to $1,700 to scholarship-granting organizations and receive a dollar-for-dollar tax credit, but students in states that do not opt in cannot benefit. The U.S. Treasury released rules in early October that lack the guardrails some Democratic governors wanted. At publication, 31 states had opted in, while most Democratic governors were undecided or opposed; only New York's Kathy Hochul and Colorado's Jared Polis had joined. Pritzker's office said he would carefully evaluate the proposed regulations.

Illinois has history here. Invest in Kids, launched in 2017, gave a 75-cent credit per dollar donated to private-school scholarship groups, and lawmakers ended it after 2023. Nearly three-quarters of its 2023 credits went to donors in Cook County or northern Illinois.

The Illinois Education Association and Illinois Federation of Teachers oppose joining, warning of strain on underfunded public schools. IEA President Karl Goeke said he hopes the governor says no. The IFT's Cyndi Oberle-Dahm warned of a possible "mass exodus" of students.

Supporters see an opportunity. The Big Shoulders Fund, which lost a major funding source when Invest in Kids ended, helped affected families, said CEO Josh Hale. St. Francis de Sales High School principal Roni Facen said donations could support financial aid and a STEM center. Stand for Children believes Illinois could maximize benefits through nonprofit infrastructure, though director Jessica Handy acknowledged the difficulty.

Illinois Families for Public Schools, with the Institute for the Public Good, concluded the program would heighten inequities because wealthier districts have more taxpayers able to claim the credit.
