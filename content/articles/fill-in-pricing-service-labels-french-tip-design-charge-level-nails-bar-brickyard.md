A returning customer shared a review of Level Nails Bar Brickyard after their second visit. They had enjoyed their first appointment enough to come back, and they planned ahead by booking online and noting the listed price of a fill-in. That kind of preparation is exactly what a salon hopes for, and it shows how much a customer values clear pricing. Their account focuses on a gap between the price they expected and the total at checkout.

## A Total That Differed From the Booking Price

The customer said that on their first visit they paid $70 for a full set of acrylic with French tips. On the second visit they came in for a fill-in and a change of French tip color, and the total was $65. They noted that the online booking page listed a fill-in at $35, so the final amount felt far higher than they had planned for.

When the price at checkout does not match what a customer saw while booking, the difference is usually the result of extras, such as a color change or a design. The customer would understandably have wanted to know about these before the service started. A quick conversation at the start, such as "A fill-in is listed at this price, and changing the tip color adds this amount. Does that work for you?" lets the customer decide with full information.

## What the Register Showed

At the register, the customer noticed the service was listed as a gel full set, and asked about it. They were told the difference from a full set was only $5. They also said a team member described the design charge as $15, and they questioned whether a French tip counts as a design.

Service labels matter because customers often read the receipt closely. A fill-in, a gel full set, and an acrylic full set are different services, and the label at checkout should match what the customer received and expected. Salons can help by training the front desk to explain each line on the bill, and by agreeing on whether a French tip color change is a design or part of the regular service.

## Making Pricing Easy to Understand

Clear price information reduces most misunderstandings. A printed or posted menu that explains what is included in a fill-in, what counts as a design, and how color changes are priced gives the team a shared reference. Online booking pages can also add a short note, such as "Color changes and designs may add to the price." When a customer raises a question at checkout, a calm explanation and, where appropriate, an adjustment, can protect the relationship.

## Editorial Perspective

This case offers several lessons. First, explain any extra charges, such as a color change or a design, before the service begins, and give the customer an estimated total. Second, make sure the service listed at checkout matches what the customer booked and received. Third, keep the online booking price, the posted menu, and the register consistent, and give the team a clear definition of what counts as a design. A returning customer who checks prices ahead of time deserves a total that matches their expectations.
