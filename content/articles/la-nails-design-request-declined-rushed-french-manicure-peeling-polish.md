A recent review of LA Nails describes a Saturday visit in which a mother and her daughter, who had been to the salon several times before, left feeling disappointed on more than one front. Her account touches on a declined design request, the way directions were given at the table, an up-front payment, and a French manicure that did not hold up past the weekend. It is a useful case for any team that wants to understand how small moments add up to a decision not to return.

## When a Design Request Gets a "No"

According to the customer, her daughter asked whether she could have half of her nails done in yellow and red and was told no. There can be sound reasons to decline a design, such as time, supplies, or what a particular technician is comfortable doing. The way the answer is delivered, though, shapes how the guest feels about the whole visit. A plain "no" with no explanation can land as dismissive, especially with a younger client who was excited about her idea.

A more welcoming approach is to share the reason briefly and offer an alternative, for example suggesting the same colors on a couple of accent nails or as a simple two-tone look. The customer also described the table communication as less than gentle: "Instead of asking to move my hand they would just point and yell." Whatever the circumstances were on a busy day, a calm, friendly request such as "Could you please shift your hand a little this way?" keeps clients at ease and costs nothing.

## Payment Up Front for Returning Guests

The reviewer noted that she was asked to "pay in advance for the service" even though the family had visited multiple times before. Collecting payment first is a legitimate policy at many salons, and there are practical reasons for it. For regular clients, however, it can feel like a signal of distrust, particularly when no explanation is offered.

Teams can avoid this by stating the policy clearly at booking or at the door, applying it consistently to every guest, and considering a flexible approach for familiar faces. A short, warm explanation ("We take payment at the start to keep things moving, thank you for understanding") goes a long way.

## A French Manicure That Felt Rushed

The service itself is where the customer felt the gap was largest. She asked for a French manicure and said her cuticles were not worked on, that only one coat was applied, and that she was sent to dry by the door with a fan, so she chose to leave. By Monday, she reported, the polish was peeling.

A durable French manicure depends on every step in order: cuticle care and nail prep, a proper base, the color or white tip in thin, even layers, and a top coat, followed by enough drying or curing time. Skipping or shortening steps, even when the salon is busy, tends to show up within days. It also helps to check in at the end of the service with a simple "How does everything look to you?" and to let clients know they are welcome to call or return if anything lifts in the first week. A quick touch-up can turn a frustrated guest into a loyal one.

## Editorial Perspective

This case shows how a visit can be judged less on one big mistake and more on a series of small ones. First, explain the reason behind a "no" and offer an alternative, so a young client's request is met with creativity instead of a closed door. Second, give directions with a calm voice and polite wording, and explain payment policies kindly and consistently, including for returning guests. Third, protect the full service routine, including cuticle care, proper coats, and adequate drying time, because rushed work is the most likely to peel and the most likely to be remembered.

Customers rarely tell a salon about these moments directly; they simply say they "won't be back." Treating each step of the visit as a chance to show care is the surest way to earn the next appointment.
