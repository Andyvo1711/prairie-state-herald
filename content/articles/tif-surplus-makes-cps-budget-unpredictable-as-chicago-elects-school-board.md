Chicago Public Schools leans heavily on tax-increment financing surpluses, and that reliance makes its budget hard to predict, according to an Oct. 19 Chalkbeat Chicago article by Alex Nitkin of the Illinois Answers Project.

TIF districts divert property-tax growth from local governments, especially CPS, into special funds for construction and public works. When mayors declare a "surplus," money returns to the taxing bodies as a one-time transfer. A surplus also gives CPS cash without triggering the state law that caps how much the district can raise its property tax levy.

The scale has grown. In 2019, the surplus was about 1% of the CPS budget. This year, CPS received more than half of Mayor Brandon Johnson's record $1 billion surplus, over 6% of its roughly $10 billion spending plan. Over five years, surpluses added between $141 million and $523 million.

The timing is awkward. CPS's fiscal year starts July 1 and the district must pass a budget months before learning the surplus amount, since the City Council finalizes its plan later. The board's current budget assumes $285 million in TIF surplus and $150 million from the state, which state leaders say Springfield does not have.

A dispute over a $175 million annual pension payment adds to the tension; last year Johnson covered it by declaring a surplus larger than CPS had budgeted. Justin Marlowe of the University of Chicago's Center for Municipal Finance said the arrangement is "bad on multiple levels."

The number of TIF districts has fallen from 160 when Richard M. Daley left office to 101 active this year, with 13 expiring this year and 15 more by 2030. The Chicago Teachers Union has long called for winding down TIFs. Kids First Chicago's Hal Woods called the surplus the opposite of sustainable, predictable revenue.

Candidates for board president offered different remedies: Hilario Dominguez called TIF a stopgap for state funding, Jessica Biggs urged more transparency, and Victor Henderson recommended conservative budgeting with multiple spending options.
