This client did what salons hope every new customer will do: he called ahead. He asked whether Tracy Michelle's Nails accepted walk-ins and what a men's manicure and pedicure with polish would cost, and he was told walk-ins were welcome and the service would be $50. According to his account, the visit then went differently from what the phone call promised, in price, in finish, and in how cared for he felt. His experience is a useful reminder of how quickly trust can slip when small details add up.

## When the Price at the Counter Doesn't Match the Phone Quote

The client says he was charged $60 for the same service he had been quoted $50 for on the phone. He decided not to argue: "I figured $10 is not a big deal… I'll let it slide." Many clients react this way, and that is exactly why the gap matters. A customer who lets a price difference go without a word is often quietly marking it down, and it shapes how he judges everything else about the visit.

A phone quote is a promise. If the final price will be different, perhaps because a specific polish or add-on costs more, staff should explain that before the service starts, not at checkout. A simple approach helps: whoever answers the phone gives the full price for exactly what the caller described, and the front desk confirms that same number when he arrives. If there is a mix-up, honoring the quoted price is usually worth far more in goodwill than the $10 difference.

## Rushing the Coats and Losing the Finish

The client said he was fine with the visit "until I seen the quality of the work on my manicure." In his description, the technician put on two coats back to back "without allowing either one to dry," and the polish ended up smeared. Whatever the reason, whether a busy schedule or simple habit, rushing between coats is one of the most common causes of streaks, bubbles, and smudges.

This is an easy area to coach. Thin coats, a short pause between each layer, and a clear topcoat step give polish a chance to level and set. Before the client leaves the table, the technician can take a moment to inspect every nail under good light and offer to fix anything that looks uneven. Men's services sometimes get treated as quick or simple, but a male client looks at his finished nails just as closely as anyone else does.

## How Every Client Feels Welcome

The client also shared an uncomfortable question: "I'm not sure if me being a man made a difference in the lack of detail and professionalism." He did not claim to know the answer, and there is no way to tell from a single review. Still, the fact that he wondered about it is worth taking seriously. When service feels hurried and a price changes without explanation, clients in any group that is less common at a salon may wonder whether they are being treated differently.

Salons can remove that doubt by being deliberately consistent. Men's manicures and pedicures should follow the same steps, timing, and quality checks as any other service, and staff should greet and check in with every client the same way. Growing the male clientele is a real business opportunity, and it depends on these clients feeling fully welcome from the first call.

## Editorial Perspective

This case leaves three practical lessons. First, treat a phone quote as a commitment and confirm the price before the service begins, so no client is surprised at the register. Second, give polish the time it needs between coats and finish every service with a careful check, because a smeared result undoes good work in seconds. Third, deliver the same attention, steps, and courtesy to every client, including men, so no one leaves wondering whether who they are affected how they were treated.
